Question 1: How do students notice that they need a break?

Students mainly identify the need for a break through cognitive performance drops and physical discomfort:

  • Cognitive Loops: Rereading the same sentence or paragraph multiple times without absorbing the meaning.

  • Accuracy Errors: Making silly or dumb mistakes in calculations or logic that they usually know how to solve.

  • Emotional Friction: Feeling disproportionately frustrated or irritated by small errors or slow progress.

  • Physical Cues: Noticing heavy eyes, stiff shoulders, a tight neck, or general restlessness.

  • Focus Drifting: Automatically doing unrelated tasks and moving in circles.

Question 2: What prevents students from taking outdoor breaks near the library?

Several points of friction discourage students from leaving the library:

  • The Darwin Climate: High heat, humidity, and the risk of standing in direct sun make the outdoors feel unappealing.

  • Security and Effort: Phyiscal effort to leave the spot and the perceived need of packing up laptops, chargers, and notes. Or the anxiety of leaving them unattended, outweighs the perceived benefit of a 5-minute break.

  • Information Gap: Students often stay inside simply because they do not know exactly where the nearest shaded, quiet seating is located.

  • Flow Preservation: Students involved in deep work (coding or theory) fear that physically leaving their spot will cause their "mental map" of the problem to collapse.

Question 3: What qualities make an outdoor campus space feel restorative rather than distracting?

For a space to provide a true reset, participants identified several essential qualities:

  • Thermal Comfort: Guaranteed shade and a breeze are non-negotiable for comfort in Darwin.

  • Quiet and Low Density: Participants find crowded or noisy areas (like busy cafe entrances) more draining than restorative, they prefer quiet, secluded spots.

  • Nature Cues: Subtle sensory inputs like trees and a breeze help with decompression, provided they are not forced.

  • Proximity: The spot must be within a 2-3 minute walk for most of them to feel like a short break rather than a time-consuming excursion.

Question 4: How do students maintain momentum when leaving and returning to study?

  • Maintaining Momentum: Momentum is best preserved by stopping at a natural completion point (e.g., after finishing a section or a set of questions).

  • What Disrupts Return: The most significant disruptor is phone usage (social media or games) during the break, which creates a "gap" that makes returning to boring academic tasks difficult.

  • What Helps Return: A one-line task note, such as 'one line before I leave' etc removes the warm-up cost and re-entry friction upon returning.

Question 5: What interaction design support would students accept, trust, and use?

Students expressed a preference for a system that is functional and low-effort rather than purely "wellness" focused:

  • The "Resume Task" Nudge: This was identified as the most valuable feature. Helping them remember exactly where they left off.

  • Actionable Map: A simple interface showing a shaded route to a confirmed nearby spot.

  • Smart Timing: Suggestions that align with their study flow rather than interrupting mid-task.

  • Shared Group Timers: For students studying in groups, a shared signal to end the break prevents one person from having to be the "annoying" one to stop the fun.

Question 6: What environmental factors make an outdoor campus space comfortable and suitable for short study breaks?

Environmental Factor Requirement for Suitability
Shade Essential. Must be proper or confirmed shade.
Seating Physical benches or chairs. Students will not take a break if they have to stand.
Breeze Highly valued for making the Darwin heat feel less heavy.
Distance Ideally 1–3 minutes away. Maximum 5 minutes total travel time.
Quietness Low background noise to allow mental relaxation.
