Thematic Analysis

The participant transcripts (P01–P10) were analysed so the themes below reflect the interview patterns across the ten participant transcripts.

Noticed Patterns
Cognitive fatigue noticed through performance breakdown
Breaks are delayed until students are already drained
Phone-based breaks are easy but hard to exit
Outdoor breaks feel like extra decision-making
Comfort certainty is required before leaving
Breaks need a clear endpoint
Return-to-task anchor supports momentum
Timing must respect study flow
Nature prompts should be light, not forced
Data use must feel optional and purposeful

Themes

T1. Students recognise break need through breakdown, not through planned self-management

Across the actual transcripts, students rarely described a planned break routine. Instead, they noticed the need for a break when study quality had already declined: re-reading without understanding, making small mistakes, getting irritated, slowing down, checking the time repeatedly, opening unrelated tabs, or feeling physically stiff and tired. This means the system should not assume students will initiate breaks early by themselves. It should help them notice early signs of fatigue without sounding judgemental or interrupting important work.

Design implication: Use gentle, student-controlled break prompts based on elapsed study time, self-reported fatigue, or natural stopping points. The language should be supportive rather than productivity-shaming.

T2. Outdoor breaks compete with easier indoor and phone-based break habits

The data shows that students often default to the easiest available break: coffee, phone scrolling, phone games, stretching near the desk, drinking water, etc. Outdoor breaks were usually seen as a better-quality reset in theory, but harder to choose in the moment because they require movement, planning, and uncertainty. For some students, the phone was not only a break tool but also a momentum disruptor because it extended the break and made study feel dull afterwards.

Design implication: The outdoor option must feel almost as easy as picking up the phone. The prototype should show one clear nearby option, not a long list, and should reduce decision-making at the moment of fatigue.

T3. Outdoor break acceptance depends on comfort certainty

Darwin heat was a consistent barrier, but the issue was more specific than “weather.” Participants wanted to know whether a space was actually shaded, close enough, seated, breezy, quiet, and worth the walk. Group study added another layer: the space needed enough room for several people, not just one bench. For students who already liked outdoor breaks, crowding and noise could make the outdoor space feel less restorative.

Design implication: Suggested spots should include concrete comfort tags: confirmed shade, seating, walking time, total break time, quiet/crowd level, breeze/heat warning, and group capacity where relevant.

T4. The central design problem is preserving the study thread

The strongest pattern across the transcripts is that students fear losing the flow/mental model of study. For theory-heavy students, this was the argument or section they were reading. For coding students, it was the mental model of files, attempted fixes, and next tests. For problem-solving students, it was the exact question or next practice task. The break itself was not always the problem. The weak handoff between leaving and returning was the problem.

Design implication: The task anchor should be treated as a core feature. Before leaving, the system should ask the student to capture one small next step, using participant examples such as "continue question five", "finish summary of article two", "check this function", or "test this query". On return, this anchor should appear immediately.

T5. Breaks need boundaries and flow-sensitive timing

Several participants wanted breaks to happen at a clear stopping point, not randomly. Others took breaks but struggled because they had no endpoint. This was especially obvious for exam cramming, outdoor breaks, and group study. Timers were useful when they created a clear finish point. shared timers were especially useful for groups because they avoided one person having to end the break socially.

Design implication: The system should support flexible timing through interface controls for reminding after the current task, starting a five-minute break, delaying until the current step is done, and sharing a group timer. Break lengths should remain short and bounded, usually around five to ten minutes.

T6. Nature support works when it doesn't feel forced or cheesy

The nature-noticing idea was not rejected, but most participants said it should not feel forced, cheesy or be too long. Students responded better to practical features: route, timing, shade, seating, and resume support. Nature prompts were acceptable when they were short and simple. Students were less likely to engage with long meditation-style or overly wellness worded prompts, especially during exams or group breaks.

Design implication: Keep nature prompts lightweight: one sentence and low effort. Examples include exact participant-style wording such as "look up from your screen", "notice what's around you", "notice the breeze", or "look up at the trees". Do not make the break feel like a formal mindfulness exercise.

T7. Trust depends on autonomy, low pressure, and minimal data use

Participants generally accepted weather and study-timer data because they clearly supported the break suggestion. Location was acceptable only when it was optional, temporary, or used while the student actively requested a nearby spot. Pushy notifications, background tracking, or unclear data collection would reduce trust and likely lead students to ignore or disable the system.

Design implication: Location should be opt-in and used only for active suggestions. The system should avoid constant tracking, avoid excessive notifications, and explain data use in plain language.
